Bikes and Trikes

The school bike rack holds bicycles and tricycles. Count the vehicles, count the wheels — how many of each?

Weekly Puzzle · #2November 25, 2026

Welcome to Puzzle #2. Last week we counted heads and legs in Grandma's pen. This week the animals have turned into wheels — but keep your eyes open: this is the same puzzle wearing a new costume. If you solved the farmyard one, you already own the tool. Let's see if you can spot where it hides.

The Puzzle

The school bike rack holds bicycles and tricycles: 9 vehicles altogether. Counting the wheels, there are 21 wheels in total.

9 vehicles · 21 wheels — how many bicycles? How many tricycles?

Every vehicle is exactly one of these: a bicycle has 2 wheels, a tricycle has 3. No spare wheels in the basket, no training wheels anywhere.

Think First, Peek Later

Don't rush to the answer. Stuck is fine — each hint you open takes you one step closer:

Hint 1: What if all 9 were bicycles?
Then the rack would hold 9 × 2 = 18 wheels. But there really are 21 — so where did the extra 21 − 18 = 3 wheels come from?
Hint 2: Swap one bicycle for one tricycle. What changes?
The number of vehicles stays at 9 — nothing changed there. But the wheels go up by 1. So those 3 extra wheels were added one at a time: 3 ÷ 1 = ?
The full solution (think first, read after)

Method 1: Make a list

Start from "all bicycles" and swap one tricycle in at a time. Every swap adds 1 wheel, until we hit exactly 21:

BicyclesTricyclesWheels
9018
7220
6321 ✓

Method 2: The assumption trick

Suppose all 9 vehicles are bicycles. That would be 9 × 2 = 18 wheels — but we counted 21, so we are 3 wheels short. Each time we trade a bicycle for a tricycle, the vehicle count stays the same and the wheel count grows by 1. So the rack holds 3 tricycles, and 9 − 3 = 6 bicycles.

21 − 9 × 2 = 3; 3 ÷ 1 = 3 tricycles; 9 − 3 = 6 bicycles

Check

6 × 2 + 3 × 3 = 12 + 9 = 21 ✓ (vehicles: 6 + 3 = 9 ✓)

So the rack holds 6 bicycles and 3 tricycles.

For Parents

This week is deliberately a repeat with a twist. Last week a rabbit-for-chicken swap added 2 legs; this week a tricycle-for-bicycle swap adds only 1 wheel. The thinking is identical — assume the simpler case, then explain the gap — but the numbers make children re-read the question instead of reusing the answer.

A great question to ask: "Why did we trade 3 vehicles today, but 5 animals last week?" The answer is that each swap buys a different amount: 1 wheel here, 2 legs there. Kids who can explain that have understood the method, not memorized it.

Solved it? Offer the boss level: the same rack, but now 12 vehicles and 28 wheels. (Answer: 8 bicycles and 4 tricycles.)
