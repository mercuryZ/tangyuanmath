The 12 Times Table

The 12 times table is the stretch goal — the table printed past the edge of most classroom charts. It is also the most "grown-up" table your child will meet: eggs come by the dozen, a year has 12 months, a foot has 12 inches, and 12 × 12 = 144 even has its own name, a gross.

The patterns in the 12s

Tens plus twos

Twelve is ten plus two, so 12 × n = (10 × n) + (2 × n). 12 × 8? Ten eights are 80, two eights are 16, total 96. Children who are solid on ×10 (the free win) and ×2 find this table almost suspiciously easy — it is built entirely from facts they own.

Or: the sixes, doubled

Because 12 = 2 × 6, every 12-answer is the matching 6-answer doubled: 6 × 9 = 54, and 12 × 9 = 108. It is the same family trick as 2 → 4 → 8, one rung further: 2, 4, 6, 12 all double into each other.

The useful neighbors: 11 and the squares

While you are up here, the 11s are nearly free (1 × 11 = 11, 2 × 11 = 22… write the digit twice for 1-digit numbers), and the big squares 12 × 12 = 144 is worth over-learning — it anchors the corner of the chart and shows up constantly in later math.

Why twelve? A little history your child can repeat

Twelve divides evenly by 2, 3, 4 and 6 — which is why traders centuries ago picked it for eggs, pencils and inches, and why "a dozen" survived. A table with that much history stops feeling like arbitrary homework and starts feeling like a piece of the world.

12 times table FAQ

Why learn the 12 times table if most charts stop at 10?

Because so much of daily life is counted in dozens — eggs, months, inches, hours on a clock face. The 12s also preview a real algebra habit: splitting 12 into 10 + 2 and multiplying in parts, exactly the break-apart move used for big numbers later.

What is the trick for the 12 times table?

Tens plus twos: 12 × n = (10 × n) + (2 × n). For 12 × 7, take 70 + 14 = 84. If the tens are ever shaky, double the sixes instead — 12 × 7 = 2 × 42.

What is 12 × 12?

144 — called "a gross" in old trading slang, the dozen-of-dozens. It is the largest fact on the classic chart and worth memorizing cold; it anchors the corner of the table and returns in geometry, factors and beyond.
