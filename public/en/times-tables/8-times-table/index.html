The 8 Times Table

The 8 times table looks intimidating and is secretly one of the most mechanical: 8 is 2 × 2 × 2, so every eight-fact is three doublings away from a number your child already knows. The eights reward children who like procedures — same move every time.

The patterns in the 8s

Double it three times

8 × n means double n, double the result, double it again. 8 × 6: 6 → 12 → 24 → 48. That is the whole table. Children who mastered the double-double of the 4 times table only need one more doubling, and it stays fast because each step is small.

The endings count down: 8, 6, 4, 2, 0

Because 8 is even, every multiple of 8 is even — and the final digits march down the even digits in order: 8, 16, 24, 32, 40, 48, 56, 64, 72, 80. Final digits 8, 6, 4, 2, 0, then the cycle repeats. Any "8-answer" that ends in an odd digit is wrong on sight.

The 8s are the 4s, doubled — and the 2s, quadrupled

Every 8-answer is the matching 4-answer doubled (4 × 7 = 28, 8 × 7 = 56) and the matching 2-answer doubled twice. The tables are a family: 2, 4 and 8 share one move at different depths. Seeing that chain is a small "math is connected" moment worth pausing for.

Spiders and octopuses

Two spiders have 16 legs (8 × 2), three octopuses have 24 arms (8 × 3). Animal legs are the classic real-life hook for eights — ask "how many legs on five spiders?" at dinner and you have a practice round disguised as a joke.

8 times table FAQ

What is the trick for the 8 times table?

Double three times: n → 2n → 4n → 8n. For 8 × 7, that is 7 → 14 → 28 → 56. Every fact in the table is three small doublings from the starting number.

Are all answers in the 8 times table even?

Yes — 8 is even, so every multiple of 8 is even. Stronger: every multiple of 8 can be halved three times over and still come out whole. The final digits cycle 8, 6, 4, 2, 0 in a strict countdown.

What are the hardest facts in the 8 times table?

The ones that overlap the 7 times table: 8 × 7 = 56 tops most teachers' lists, with 8 × 12 = 96 a distant second. Both respond well to the triple-double trick — 7 → 14 → 28 → 56 and 12 → 24 → 48 → 96.
