The 7 Times Table

The 7 times table has a reputation, and it has earned it: 7 is the one number from 2 to 9 with no doubling trick, no fives shortcut, no finger method. But the reputation overstates the problem — most of what looks like "the hard 7s" is facts your child already owns from other tables, wearing a 7 on the left.

The patterns in the 7s

Why the sevens feel patternless

Look down the answers: 7, 14, 21, 28, 35, 42, 49, 56, 63, 70. The final digits run 7, 4, 1, 8, 5, 2, 9, 6, 3, 0 — every digit exactly once, in an order that refuses to repeat early. Compare the easy tables (2s alternate even digits, 5s end in 5 or 0, 9s climb then fall) and you can see why 7 feels random: its unit digits genuinely have the least rhythm of any table.

Most of the 7s are other tables in disguise

Count what is actually new. 1 × 7 through 5 × 7 are facts from the easy tables (2 × 7, 3 × 7, 4 × 7, 5 × 7 — owned already). The swap rule means 6 × 7 is the sixes' problem too. That leaves genuinely new facts: 7 × 7, 7 × 8, 7 × 9, 7 × 10, 7 × 11, 7 × 12 — six facts, not twelve. Framing it this way makes the table feel half its size.

Break the 7 apart: 5 + 2

The one reliable trick is break-apart: 7 = 5 + 2, so 7 × n = (5 × n) + (2 × n). 7 × 8? Five eights are 40, two eights are 16, total 56. It uses the two best-owned tables (5s and 2s) and it is exactly the break-apart strategy from Lesson 7 of our starter course.

Two set-piece facts worth over-learning

7 × 7 = 49 (the near-halfway square) and 7 × 8 = 56 — the single most-forgotten fact in the chart. 56 has a gift in it: the answer is the factors written in a row, 5, 6, 7, 8. Say it as "five six seven eight — 56 = 7 × 8" a few times and it stops falling out.

7 times table FAQ

Why is the 7 times table considered the hardest?

Seven is prime and small, so it has no useful decomposition: not a double of anything, not part of the fives, no finger shortcut like the nines. Its unit digits also cycle through all ten digits with no early repetition, so skip-counting the sevens has less rhythm than any other table.

What is the best trick for the 7 times table?

Break the 7 into 5 + 2: 7 × n = (5 × n) + (2 × n). For 7 × 9, that is 45 + 18 = 63. It relies only on the fives and twos, which most children know cold, and it generalizes to every fact in the table.

What is 7 × 8?

56. It is the most-forgotten fact in the whole multiplication chart — and it has a built-in memory hook: the digits 5, 6, 7, 8 appear in order, so "five, six, seven, eight" walks you straight to 56 = 7 × 8.
