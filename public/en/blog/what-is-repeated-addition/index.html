What Is Repeated Addition in Math?

5 + 5 + 5 is more than a long addition — it is the first step toward 3 × 5. Examples, mix-ups, and practice for parents.

November 17, 2026

Your child brings home a worksheet that says “Use repeated addition to solve 3 × 4” — and suddenly a multiplication question turns into an adding question. This guide is for that moment. Repeated addition is the bridge between the adding your child already knows and the multiplication coming next, and it is easier to explain at the kitchen table than you might expect.

Here is the short version: repeated addition means adding the same number, again and again. 4 + 4 + 4 is repeated addition. So is 6 + 6. The trick is that the same number keeps repeating — and whenever a number repeats like that, a multiplication is hiding inside.

What Is Repeated Addition?

Repeated addition is adding equal groups together. Take one number and add it a certain number of times:

4 + 4 + 4 = 12

Three fours. The same number — 4 — shows up three times, and the answer is the total of all of them. That is all repeated addition is.

The name matters. Ordinary addition can join any numbers: 4 + 9, 2 + 7, whatever comes. Repeated addition is special because one number repeats. If the numbers are all different — 4 + 9 + 3 — that is honest addition, but it is not repeated addition, and no multiplication shortcut is hiding inside it.

Repeated Addition in Real Life

Children meet repeated addition long before worksheets do the naming. It hides in the kitchen, in drawers, and by the front door:

  • Two egg cartons: 6 + 6 = 12 eggs.
  • A muffin tin: 4 + 4 + 4 = 12 cups.
  • Sticker sheets: 5 + 5 + 5 + 5 = 20 stickers.
  • Pairs of shoes: 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 = 8 shoes.
  • Wheels on tricycles: 3 + 3 + 3 = 9 wheels.

Read any of these out loud with your child and ask the magic question: “What number keeps showing up?” When the same number repeats, you have found the group — and counting how many times it shows up gives you the other number.

From 5 + 5 + 5 to 3 × 5

Here is where multiplication enters — not as a new idea, but as a shorthand. Writing 5 + 5 + 5 takes a while, and mathematicians are famously fond of shortcuts, so they invented a shorter way to say it:

3 × 5 = 5 + 5 + 5 = 15

Read 3 × 5 as “3 groups of 5”: there are 3 groups, and each group holds 5. The number of groups comes first, the size of each group comes second.

Both 3 × 5 and 5 × 3 equal 15 — the reason comes a little further down. But when a worksheet says “use repeated addition for 3 × 5”, write the matching sentence: 5 + 5 + 5.

From Repeated Addition to Arrays

Lay those groups out in neat rows and you get the picture that powers early multiplication classrooms: the array.

An array is repeated addition you can see: each row is one equal group. Three rows of five dots is 5 + 5 + 5, which is 3 × 5, which is 15. Same idea, three costumes.

Arrays also explain where the two numbers of a multiplication come from: count the rows going down, count the dots in one row going across. Our guide to rows and columns in arrays walks through that picture step by step, with dot diagrams you can copy at home.

When Does Repeated Addition Stop Working?

Repeated addition is a wonderful first step — and it has a built-in ceiling. Ask your child to solve 8 × 7 by writing 7 + 7 + 7 + 7 + 7 + 7 + 7 + 7, and watch enthusiasm run out somewhere around the fourth seven.

This is exactly where the journey is supposed to go. Repeated addition builds the meaning of multiplication; then skip counting — 7, 14, 21, 28… — makes finding the total faster; and eventually the facts get remembered, so 8 × 7 snaps out with no adding at all. Lesson 3 of the free Multiplication Starter Course turns that row-by-row counting into jumps of 5, 10, and 7.

The point worth making for your child: multiplication was never about doing lots of adding. It is about seeing the groups — and then getting faster than adding.

Common Mix-ups (and Quick Fixes)

1. Writing 3 + 5 instead of 3 × 5

The most common slip on worksheets. 3 × 5 means 5 + 5 + 5 — the 3 counts how many groups there are, it is not one of the numbers being added. Quick fix: circle the groups first. Once your child can point to three groups of five, writing 5 + 5 + 5 comes naturally. Our rows and columns guide shows the pointing trick on a dot picture.

2. Counting the groups backwards

2 + 2 + 2 gets read as “two twos”, and some children write 2 × 2. The reliable habit: count how many times the number repeats. 2 + 2 + 2 has three twos, so it is 2 × 3 = 6. Say “three groups of two” and the first number takes care of itself.

3. Adding unequal groups

5 + 5 + 4 looks close to repeated addition, but the last group breaks the pattern — there is no multiplication fact for it. Have your child check that every group matches before reaching for ×. (Later this becomes “two fives and one more four”: 2 × 5 + 4. In the early stages, honest addition is fine.)

Frequently Asked Questions

Is repeated addition the same as multiplication?

For whole numbers, yes — multiplication means repeated addition, and the × sign is just the shorthand. (Much later, multiplication grows into ideas repeated addition cannot picture — halves of halves, negatives — but that is years away. At the start, they are the same thing said twice.)

Why do teachers start with repeated addition?

Because it starts from what children can already do. Adding is familiar; the × sign is not. Writing 5 + 5 + 5 first lets a child earn the shorthand instead of memorizing it — and “equal groups” becomes something they can point to, not a rule to recite.

What comes after repeated addition?

Arrays. Lining the groups into rows and columns gives every fact a picture — and turns one fact into two, because 3 × 5 and 5 × 3 are the same dots. Our guide to what rows and columns in arrays mean is the natural next read. From there: skip counting, then the times tables.

Is 2 × 6 “two sixes” or “six twos”?

Both equal 12, and your child will hear both. The usual classroom reading follows the shorthand above: the first number counts the groups — 2 × 6 is “2 groups of 6”, written 6 + 6. Stay relaxed about it: flipping the numbers changes the sentence, not the total.
