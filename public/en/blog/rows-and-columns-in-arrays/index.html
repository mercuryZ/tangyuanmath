What Are Rows and Columns in Arrays?

Rows go across, columns go up and down — and together they turn one grid of dots into two multiplication facts.

November 3, 2026

If your child's worksheet shows a neat grid of dots and asks “How many rows? How many columns?” — this guide is for you. Rows and columns are the two directions of an array, and once your child can tell them apart, every multiplication fact gets a picture to hold on to.

Here is the short version: rows go across (left to right), columns go up and down (top to bottom). Rows are read like a line of text; columns stand like pillars. Count the rows going down, count the dots in one row going across, and you have a multiplication.

First, What Is an Array?

An array is simply objects lined up in equal rows. Egg cartons, muffin tins, window panes, and sticker sheets are all arrays. In math lessons we usually draw them as dots.

The one rule that makes an array an array: every row has the same number of items. If one row has 4 dots and another has 5, it is just a scattered pile — and there is no multiplication shortcut for a scattered pile.

Rows Go Across

A row runs from left to right, like a row of seats in a movie theater, or the line your eyes travel when reading a line of text.

In this array, each row has 5 dots. Slide a finger across one row and you count 5 — not 15. A row is one equal group.

Columns Go Up and Down

A column runs from top to bottom, like the columns that hold up a building, or the columns of digits lined up in a vertical addition.

A handy phrase to repeat at home: rows read across, columns stand tall.

How Rows and Columns Become a Multiplication

An array hands you two numbers for free:

  1. Count the rows — move your finger down the side: 3 rows.
  2. Count one row — move your finger across: 5 dots.

“3 rows of 5” is written as a multiplication:

3 × 5 = 15

And 3 × 5 is just a short way to write repeated addition:

5 + 5 + 5 = 15

Counting row by row — 5, 10, 15 — is skip counting. That is why arrays and skip counting are taught together: each row literally is one jump.

Turn the Array, Get a Second Fact for Free

Now look at the same array from its side. What was 3 rows of 5 becomes 5 rows of 3:

Same dots, same total: 5 × 3 = 15. One picture proves two facts at once — 3 × 5 = 5 × 3. That is the commutative property of multiplication, and arrays turn it into something your child can see instead of memorize.

In 3 × 7, Which Number Is the Rows?

When a problem says “the array shows 3 × 7”, read it as 3 rows of 7 — rows first is the common classroom reading. But do not worry: because the answer is the same either way, nothing breaks if your child flips the order.

What matters at this stage is that one number tells how many equal groups there are (the rows) and the other tells how many are in each group (the dots in a row).

Arrays Are Everywhere

  • An egg carton: 2 rows of 6 eggs — that's 2 × 6 = 12.
  • A muffin tin: 3 rows of 4 cups — 3 × 4 = 12.
  • A calendar: 7 columns, and each column is the same weekday going down the weeks.
  • The multiplication table itself is a giant array: to find 7 × 8, go down to row 7 and across to column 8.
  • A 4 × 6 rectangle tiled with squares is 4 rows of 6 — arrays grow into the area model of multiplication.

Common Mix-ups (and Quick Fixes)

1. “Which one is the row again?”

Rows read across like a line of text; columns stand tall like pillars. Repeat the phrase until it sticks.

2. Counting rows by counting dots

Asked “how many rows?”, the answer is 3 — not 15. Have your child point a finger down, touching one whole row at a time, and only count dots when moving across.

3. Writing 3 + 5 instead of 3 × 5

Addition merges two different amounts. Multiplication repeats the same amount. Three rows of 5 dots is 5 + 5 + 5 — not 3 + 5.

4. Treating uneven rows as an array

One row of 4 and one row of 5 is not an array. No equal rows means no shortcut — just honest addition: 4 + 5.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a row vertical or horizontal?

Horizontal. Rows run left to right across the page; columns run top to bottom.

In 3 × 4, is the 3 the rows or the columns?

Usually the rows — “3 rows of 4”. Even flipped to 4 rows of 3, the total is the same. The habit worth building: one number means “how many equal rows”, the other means “how many dots in each row”.

Do rows or columns come first?

Early multiplication usually writes rows first, which matches most worksheets. Later, different settings use different conventions (spreadsheets, grid references). Agree on “rows first” at home, and stay relaxed — the same array always tells you both facts.

Why do arrays matter beyond dots?

Arrays grow into the area model, the multiplication table, and later the rows and columns of tables and spreadsheets. They also make the commutative property visible: turn the picture, and the fact stays true.
