Lesson 4 · 15 min Stage 2 · Rules that shrink the work

The swap trick

3 × 4 and 4 × 3 are the same dots, turned sideways. That one idea quietly erases almost half the times table.

In this lesson your child will see — not just be told — that swapping the two numbers never changes the total, and will start collecting "free twins" for every fact they learn.

Talk it through

Say

"Yesterday you asked a great question: is 3 rows of 4 written 3 × 4 or 4 × 3? Today we settle it forever — and the answer makes your life easier."

Do

Build 3 rows × 4 columns in the demo below. Count the dots: 12. Now the key question: "If I turn this whole array sideways — if I pick it up and rotate it — how many dots do I have now?" Let your child guess. Then press the turn button.

Say

"Four rows of 3! But nobody put dots on or took dots off — it's the same array, just turned. Same dots, same total. So 3 × 4 = 4 × 3. Always. Swapping the numbers never changes the answer."

Say

"Here's why you should care: every fact you learn is secretly two facts. The day you learn 3 × 4 = 12, you get 4 × 3 = 12 as a gift. Free. No extra memorizing."

Do

Hunt for a swap in the kitchen: an egg carton is 2 rows of 6 — or 6 columns of 2. A chocolate bar, a window with panes, a muffin tin. Say both versions out loud for each.

Try it together

Build any array, predict the total, then press Turn it! and predict again — the total never moves. Try 5 × 2, 8 × 3, and one big one like 10 × 4.

Turn the array — watch the total refuse to change

Quick check

Common mix-ups

  • "But the story is different!" Right — and that's fine. 3 plates of 4 crackers and 4 plates of 3 crackers are different stories that end with the same number of crackers. The swap changes the story, never the total.
  • Assuming every operation swaps. Adding does (3 + 4 = 4 + 3), multiplying does — but subtracting and dividing do not (5 − 2 ≠ 2 − 5). Worth one sentence now; it saves confusion later.
  • "Big numbers first feels wrong." Some children think 12 × 3 must be bigger than 3 × 12. It never is. Turn the array and the doubt dies.

Play it now

Time for the full 1–10 table for the first time — the swap rule means it's already half as big as it looks. Ask your child to notice when the game serves a fact they already "own" sideways.

Practice the full 1–10 table
Parent tip: use the "instant twin" move when quizzing: they answer 3 × 4 = 12, you immediately ask "so 4 × 3?" — and they get to feel fast. That feeling, repeated daily, is how confidence compounds.
