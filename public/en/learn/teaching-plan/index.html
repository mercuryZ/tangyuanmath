Lesson 9 · 10 min Stage 4 · For you, the parent

Your 15-minute coaching plan

The lessons built the understanding. This plan turns it into mastery — without tears, without marathon sessions.

In this lesson — for you, not your child — you'll get the daily rhythm, the practice ladder, the signals for moving on or stepping back, and the four mistakes that most often slow children down.

The daily 5-5-5

Fifteen minutes, once a day. Same time helps — after school snack, before dinner, whatever slot your family can defend. The shape:

  • 5 minutes — warm and celebrate. Skip-count a chant (2s, 5s, 10s), rapid-fire the free facts, or ask them to "teach" you yesterday's rule. Warm-ups are for confidence, not challenge.
  • 5 minutes — one idea. Revisit one lesson demo, or work 2–3 facts from the famous ten with break-apart. One idea per day; depth over coverage.
  • 5 minutes — a game round. One round in Practice at today's rung of the ladder (below). End on a win if you can — stop at a good round, not a bad one.

Little and often beats big and rare: ten minutes daily is worth more than an hour on Sunday, because memory grows during the gaps, not during the session.

The practice ladder

Climb one rung at a time. Each link lands in the game preset to that range:

  • Rung 1 — facts to 5 × 5 (Lessons 1–2)
  • Rung 2 — the 2–5 facts (Lesson 3)
  • Rung 3 — full 1–10 table (Lessons 4–5)
  • Rung 4 — the nines row (Lesson 6)
  • Rung 5 — the 3–9 core (Lesson 7)
  • Rung 6 — the famous ten's home turf (Lesson 8)

When to climb, when to wait

  • Climb when: first-try accuracy is around 8 out of 10, on two different days. Not one lucky round — two.
  • Wait when: they're still figuring most facts with break-apart. Figuring is a legitimate stage — let the strategy do its work; speed arrives on its own.
  • Step back one rung when: frustration shows (any rung, any day). Drop back, win easily, end early. A stepped-back day that ends happy is worth ten pushed days that end in tears.

When a round goes badly, the game quietly collects the missed facts — Practice My Mistakes (on the game's home screen) replays exactly those. Once or twice a week, swap the day's round for a mistakes round.

Pencil and paper, sometimes

Once or twice a week, one printed page beats a screen: our printable times table for the fridge (cross out the famous ten as they become automatic), and worksheets when you want written practice. Writing by hand slows the hand down to the speed of thought — that's a feature.

The four classic mistakes

  • Speed too early. Timed pressure before facts are solid teaches children to guess fast. Untimed figuring first; speed is a by-product, not a goal.
  • Sessions too long. After 15–20 minutes, a young child's return on effort collapses — and tomorrow's willingness collapses with it. Quit while it's fun.
  • Comparison. "Your cousin already knows her sevens" — the fastest way to make math feel like a judgment. The only comparison that helps: last week's self.
  • Treating strategies as cheating. Fingers, break-apart, the ×10 recipe — these are how memory is built, not avoided. Celebrate the figuring; the facts will settle in.

What mastery looks like

Not perfection. Mastery is: the free facts instant, the rest answerable within a breath — some recalled, some built from rules — and, most of all, your child believing multiplication is something they can do. When the 1–10 table feels boring, the game's Challenge mode and bigger ranges (up to 2-digit × 2-digit) are waiting, along with our mental-math tricks for bigger numbers.

The real finish line: one evening your child will answer 7 × 8 without a pause and without noticing it was ever hard. You'll notice. That's the moment this course was built for. Enjoy it.
