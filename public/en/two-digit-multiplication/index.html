Two-digit multiplication, built the right way

Two-digit multiplication is not a new subject — it is times tables, used twice, plus careful adding. This page shows the honest order to learn it in, the one method that always works, one famous shortcut and its strict conditions, and the mistakes to watch for. Every practice link below is free and needs no account.

First, be honest about the prerequisite

If the 1–10 times tables are not yet fluent, two-digit work will feel like wading through mud. That is not a motivation problem — it is a missing foundation. Fix it first: our free 9-lesson starter course takes a child from "what does × mean?" to the whole table, and a 1–10 practice round shows where your child stands today.

The method that always works: break apart

Split one number into tens and ones, multiply each piece, add the results. It works for every pair of numbers, forever.

23 × 7 = 20 × 7 + 3 × 7 = 140 + 21 = 161

For two-digit × two-digit, do it twice:

23 × 14 = 23 × 10 + 23 × 4 = 230 + 92 = 322

The written column method and this mental method are the same idea wearing different clothes — each partial product is one "piece". Children who can break numbers apart understand the column method instead of merely performing it.

The famous shortcut — and its strict conditions

When the tens digits match and the units add to 10, there is a lovely trick:

23 × 27 → front: 2 × 3 = 6, back: 3 × 7 = 21 → 621

Both conditions must hold. 23 × 28? Units make 11, not 10 — the trick does not apply; break apart instead. Our mental-math tips page covers this and five other tricks, each with its conditions spelled out.

Three mistakes to watch for

  • Place-value slips. 23 × 4 computed as 812 (digits glued) instead of 92. The fix is talking through the pieces: twenty-threes times four, then three times four.
  • Using the shortcut without checking conditions. The #1 source of "trick answers" that are confidently wrong. Conditions first, trick second.
  • Racing before understanding. Timed drills on a shaky method train fast guessing. Practice untimed until the method is solid — speed follows on its own.
